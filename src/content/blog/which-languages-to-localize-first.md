Once you can translate a video into another language without reshooting, the question changes from "can we" to "which first." Reelay's [Translate & lipsync](/platform/translate) offers 190 target languages and regional variants, and several can render in parallel, so the limit is not the tool. The limit is your attention: every language you add is a market you have to serve, a page you have to localize, a support inbox you have to read. Here is a way to choose that does not rely on guesswork.

## Start from where the demand already is

The best signal is the one you already have. Look at your own numbers for the last few months:

- **Where orders come from.** Countries with a steady trickle of orders despite a video in the wrong language are the first candidates. People are already buying through friction; removing the friction is cheap.
- **Where visitors come from but do not buy.** Traffic without conversion often means the message is not landing. Language is one plausible reason.
- **Where your ads are already running.** If you spend on a market, the creative should speak its language.
- **What your support inbox says.** Questions that arrive in another language are customers telling you which one.

Make a short list from these four. It is usually three to six countries, and it is usually different from the list you would have written from intuition.

## Language is not the same as country

A country can have several languages, and a language can have several regional variants. Both matter when you pick targets.

- Some countries have two or more widely spoken languages. Shipping to one of them with a single translation can mean speaking to only part of the audience.
- A language spoken in several countries differs by region in vocabulary, pronunciation and sometimes spelling. A viewer notices when the voice sounds like it comes from somewhere else, even when every word is understood.

Reelay's target list includes regional variants, so you can pick the variant that fits the market rather than a generic version. For a first round, choose the variant of the country you actually ship to. If you later expand to another country with the same language, add that variant then; several languages render in parallel from the same upload, so adding one is a few minutes, not a new project.

## Weigh the market, then weigh the fit

With a short list in hand, score each candidate on two axes.

**Market size for your product.** Not the population; the number of people who buy the kind of thing you sell, at your price, online. A small country with high online spending can beat a large one with low spending.

**Fit of the existing video.** Some videos translate cleanly: a presenter talking about a product with no on-screen text and no cultural references. Others do not: wordplay in the hook, a price in a currency the market does not use, a seasonal reference that is off by six months, a product that is sold under a different name there.

Rank by size, then move anything with a bad fit down the list until you have fixed the video or decided to make a separate one. The [localization checklist for product videos](/blog/localization-checklist-for-product-videos) covers what to look for.

## Consider what each language costs you after the video

The translation itself is the smallest part of entering a market. Before you add a language, ask what else has to exist:

- A product page in that language, or at least a checkout the viewer can complete.
- Prices in a currency they recognize.
- Shipping and returns that make sense for that country.
- Someone who can read a support e-mail in that language, even if the answer comes from a translation tool.

A video that drives people to a page they cannot read is a wasted render. The order to localize in is often the order in which you can realistically support customers, not the order of market size.

## A practical sequence

Here is a sequence that works for most small and mid-sized shops:

1. **One language, one market.** Pick the top of your list. Translate your best-performing video, not your newest one. Run it where you already have some demand.
2. **Check the result the way a native speaker would.** Reelay's automatic quality check compares every translated line with the original for missing or added words, meaning, numbers, names and natural phrasing, and corrects within the timing. Add your brand name and any product terms to the glossary so they stay as they are. Still, have one person who speaks the language watch it before you spend on it. The check catches the mistakes a machine can catch; a person catches the ones that are merely odd.
3. **Add two or three more at once.** When the first market behaves as expected, add the next few in parallel. Rendering several languages from one upload takes minutes; the thinking in step one is what took time.
4. **Hold the long tail until the pages exist.** Languages beyond the first handful are cheap to render and expensive to serve. Add them when the page and the support are ready, not before.

## Speed or Precision, by language

Reelay offers two lip-sync modes. Speed is standard lip-sync; Precision redraws the mouth and is slower and pricier. Which to pick can depend on the language pair. Languages with a rhythm and mouth shapes close to the source often look fine in Speed. A pair where the new language runs much longer or uses very different sounds can benefit from Precision, especially in close-up footage where the mouth is large on screen.

A sensible rule: use Speed for the first pass in every language, watch the results, and re-render in Precision only the languages and the videos where the mouth draws attention to itself. The form shows the estimate for each mode before you start, and the project page shows the real amount afterwards, so you can see what the upgrade costs per language.

## Keep one video, many versions

The reason this is worth doing at all is that you shoot once. The speaker's own voice is kept and the lips are matched to each new language, so each version looks like it was made for that market. The article [one video, ten languages, no reshoot](/blog/one-video-ten-languages-no-reshoot) explains what happens to the video along the way.

Treat your source video as an asset you keep improving. When you fix something in the original (a clearer hook, a tighter ending), the fix flows into every language on the next render. That is also an argument for keeping the source video clean: no burned-in text, captions added at translation time rather than in the original, and a speaker who looks at the camera.

## A short decision checklist

- Which countries already order, visit, or write to us in another language?
- Which language, and which regional variant, does each of those actually use?
- Is the existing video a good fit for that market, or does something in it need to change?
- Does a page, a price and a support path exist for that language?
- Which single language goes first, and which best-performing video?
- Who will watch the result before we spend on it?

Answer those and the list writes itself. The tool will keep up; the question is only how many markets you are ready to look after.
