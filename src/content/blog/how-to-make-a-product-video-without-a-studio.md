Most product videos are not made in a studio. They are made at a kitchen table, in a stockroom, or on a laptop between two other tasks. The studio version looks nicer, but it is not what makes the video work. What makes it work is that someone sees the product clearly, hears one useful thing about it, and knows what to do next. You can get all three without renting a single light.

This article walks through what a product video actually needs, the three ways people usually make one, and the route we built Reelay for: one photo, two or three sentences, a finished vertical ad.

## What a product video actually needs

Strip away the production and a short product video has four parts:

1. **A clear look at the product.** Not a slideshow, not a montage. One good view, held long enough to register.
2. **A voice saying something useful.** What problem it solves, for whom, and why this one.
3. **A reason to act now.** A spoken line that tells the viewer where to go.
4. **Captions.** Many people scroll with the sound off. If the words are not on screen, they are not heard.

Everything else is finish. Lighting, a second camera angle, a model holding the product: these raise the ceiling, but they do not change the floor. A video that gets the four parts right will do its job in a feed.

## Three ways to make one

### Film it yourself

A phone, a window, a table. This is cheap and honest, and it is how a lot of good content starts. The cost is time: setting up, retaking, finding the clip where you did not stumble over the word, trimming, adding captions, exporting in the right size. The first video takes an afternoon. The tenth is faster, but it never becomes quick.

### Hire someone

A creator or a small agency will shoot and edit for you. You write a brief, wait, review, ask for changes, wait again. The result is usually better than your own footage. The cost is money and calendar time, and both grow with every revision. For a product you are still testing, that is a heavy bet. We looked at the full picture of time and money in [what a video ad costs](/blog/what-a-video-ad-costs-in-time-and-money).

### Generate it from a photo

The third route skips the camera. You give one product photo and a short description, and the video is written, voiced, cut and captioned for you. This is what [Image to video](/platform/ads) in Reelay does. It will not film your product in someone's hands, and it will not add music or animated text, because it does not do those things. For most e-commerce products that is exactly the floor described above, reached in minutes.

## The no-studio route, step by step

Here is what the process looks like when you make the video from a photo.

### 1. Pick the photo

One photo is enough, and the choice matters more than the quantity. A sharp image, the whole product visible, some room around it, an even light. A clean packshot works. A photo of the product in use works too. A dark, cropped or busy photo does not get better when it moves. [Product photos that make a good product clip](/blog/product-photos-that-make-a-good-product-clip) goes into the details.

### 2. Write two or three sentences

Not a spec sheet. Say what the product is, who it is for, and what changes for them. The script is written from this description, so the quality of the sentences becomes the quality of the words the presenter says. If you want to see what a good description looks like against a weak one, read [the product description guide](/resources/guides/product-description-that-makes-a-good-script).

### 3. Choose a length

Reelay offers 10, 20, 30, 45 or 60 seconds, with 30 as the default. The script has a hard word limit of about 2.5 words per second, so a 30-second ad holds about 75 spoken words. That is enough for a hook, a problem, the product, two benefits and a call to action. A 10-second ad holds 25 words: a hook, the product, the call to action, and nothing else.

### 4. Pick the presenter and the tone

Choose a presenter, a language, an accent and a voice, and set the speaking speed. Choose a tone: elegant, playful, direct or warm. If you have a deal, type it in the Offer line; that is the only way a deal gets into the call to action. If you want the script to lean a certain way, the Direction field takes a sentence about the audience or the emphasis.

### 5. Review the script, or do not

Tick "Review script first" and the process pauses after the script is written. You see three hooks, pick one, and edit any word before the presenter or the product clip is made. Skip the tick and the first hook is used. For your first few videos, review. You will learn fast what your descriptions produce.

## A worked example

Take an invented product: a ceramic pour-over coffee dripper with a narrow channel that slows the pour.

The description a shop owner might type:

> A ceramic pour-over dripper with a narrower channel than most, so water sits a few seconds longer on the grounds. For people who brew at home and find their coffee flat or bitter. Fits any standard mug.

A 30-second script written from that, within 75 words, could read:

> Your coffee tastes flat, and it is not the beans. Most drippers pour too fast and pull out the bitterness first. This dripper has a narrower channel, so the water sits a few seconds longer and the sweetness comes through. It is ceramic, so it holds its heat, and it fits any standard mug. Try one brew side by side with your old dripper. The link is below.

Hook, problem, product, benefits, call to action, in that order, every word spoken by the presenter and shown in the captions. Nothing on screen but the presenter, the product clip and those captions.

## What makes a no-studio video look cheap

It is rarely the absence of a studio. It is usually one of these:

- **A weak photo.** Blurry, cropped, shot under a yellow bulb. The clip inherits all of it.
- **A description that lists features.** "Ceramic, 300 ml, dishwasher safe" produces a script that lists features. Write about the problem and the person.
- **Too many words for the length.** When the script is cut to fit, something good goes missing. Keep the description focused so the important things survive.
- **The wrong format.** A landscape video in a vertical feed is a small picture with large empty bars. Reelay only makes 9:16, so this one is handled for you.
- **A hidden call to action.** If the viewer has to guess what to do, they scroll. Say it out loud in the last sentence.

## When you do want a studio

There are products where footage of real use is the whole argument: a tool that cuts something, a fabric that drapes, a device that changes state. For those, filmed footage earns its cost, and a generated product video is a good first test before you spend on it. For everything that can be understood from a clear photo and a clear sentence, which is most of what sells online, the studio is optional.
