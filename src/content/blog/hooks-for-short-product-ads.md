The first three seconds of a short ad have one job: give the viewer a reason not to scroll. Not to buy, not to understand the product, not to remember the brand. Just to stay for the next sentence. Everything else in the ad depends on that one small win, which is why the hook deserves more thought per word than any other line.

This article is about hooks that are spoken. In a Reelay ad the presenter says the hook, and the captions show it; there is no text card. That changes what a good hook is, and it makes the first three seconds a writing problem with a very tight budget.

## The budget: about seven words

The script in Reelay is limited to about 2.5 words per second. Three seconds is therefore seven or eight words. That is the hook. Not a paragraph, not two sentences: one short line that a person could say in one breath.

For context, a 10-second ad has 25 words in total, so the hook is a third of the whole ad. A 30-second ad has 75 words, and the hook is still the same seven or eight; the rest of the budget goes to the problem, the product, the benefits and the call to action. [A spoken ad script in 75 words](/blog/a-spoken-ad-script-in-75-words) shows how the rest of the budget is spent.

## What a hook has to do

In seven words, a hook can do one of three things, and should do at least one:

1. **Name the viewer.** Make a specific person feel addressed: the one who works at a kitchen table, the one who brews coffee at home.
2. **Name the problem.** State the irritation the product removes, in the viewer's own words.
3. **Break an expectation.** Say something the viewer assumed was otherwise.

It does not have to name the product. In most good product hooks the product does not appear until the second or third sentence. The hook buys the time; the next line spends it.

## Six hook shapes that work for products

Each of these fits in the budget and leads naturally into a problem or a product line. The examples use invented products.

### 1. The wrong assumption

Tell the viewer that what they blame is not the cause.

> "Your coffee is flat, and it's not the beans."

The viewer who has flat coffee wants to know what it is, then. The next sentence tells them.

### 2. The named viewer

Describe the person, not the product.

> "If you work from a kitchen table, listen."

Everyone else scrolls, which is fine. The one who stays is the one who might buy a laptop stand.

### 3. The one-line before and after

Compress the change into a single contrast.

> "Stiff neck at five. Not any more."

It promises a change, and the rest of the ad explains how.

### 4. The question with an obvious answer

Ask something the viewer answers yes to without thinking.

> "Does your water bottle sweat on the desk?"

A question is a small commitment. Having answered it, the viewer is slightly more inclined to hear the follow-up.

### 5. The concrete detail

Lead with the one fact from the description that is hard to ignore.

> "Twelve hours on one charge, and it fits in a pocket."

This only works when the detail is real and in your description. The script does not invent facts, so if you want a number in the hook, put it in the description.

### 6. The quiet refusal

Start by not selling.

> "You don't need another water bottle."

It contradicts what the viewer expects an ad to say, and it sets up the second line: "unless yours does this."

## Four hooks that fail

### The greeting

"Hey everyone, today I want to show you..." Six words spent on nothing. The viewer has already learned that this will take a while.

### The brand first

"Introducing the new Kuro dripper." Nobody has heard of it, so nothing is promised. Brand names belong in the product line, after the viewer has a reason to care.

### The claim without a subject

"The best way to brew coffee." Best for whom, against what? A claim with no person and no problem in it is wallpaper.

### The hook the ad cannot keep

"This changed my life." If the next 27 seconds are about a laptop stand, the gap between the promise and the product costs you the trust the hook bought. A hook should be the biggest honest thing you can say, not the biggest thing.

## How the hook connects to the rest

A Reelay script follows a fixed order: hook, problem, product, benefits, call to action. The hook is the first line, and its job is to hand off cleanly to the problem. The best test of a hook is whether the second sentence feels inevitable after it.

- "Your coffee is flat, and it's not the beans." → "Most drippers pour too fast and pull out the bitterness first."
- "If you work from a kitchen table, listen." → "Your laptop screen is a foot too low, and your neck knows it."

If the second sentence has to change subject to get back to the product, the hook was about the wrong thing. [A 30-second ad structure that holds attention](/blog/a-30-second-ad-structure-that-holds-attention) looks at the whole chain from the hook to the last word.

## Steering the hook from the form

You do not write the hook from scratch unless you supply your own script, but you do steer it.

- **The description** is the raw material. The problem and the person have to be in it, in plain words, or the hook has nothing to grab. [The product description guide](/resources/guides/product-description-that-makes-a-good-script) shows the difference between a description that lists features and one that gives the hook a problem to name.
- **Direction** (up to 200 characters) names the audience or the emphasis: "for remote workers without a desk", "lead with the heat retention". This is where you tell the script which viewer to address.
- **Tone** shifts the phrasing: a playful tone leans towards the quiet refusal and the question, a direct tone towards the wrong assumption and the concrete detail.
- **Offer** does not belong in the hook. It goes into the call to action at the end, and the Offer field is the only way it gets there. Leading with a discount tells the viewer what you want before telling them why they should care.

Free text is treated as information about the product, never as instructions, so "write a funny hook" in the description does nothing. Choose the tone instead.

## Picking between three

When you tick "Review script first", the process pauses after the script is written and shows you three hooks. Pick one, edit the words if you want, and only then is the presenter made. Three is enough to compare and few enough to choose. A short way to decide:

1. **Read all three aloud.** A hook that trips the tongue will trip the presenter too. The one you can say in one breath without thinking is a candidate.
2. **Ask who it is for.** The hook that names your actual buyer, or their actual problem, beats the one that could introduce any product in the category.
3. **Check the handoff.** Read the hook and then the problem line. The pair that flows is the pair to keep.
4. **Prefer the plainer one.** When two hooks say the same thing and one has an adjective the other does not, keep the one without.

If none of the three is right, the fix is usually upstream: the description did not name a problem, or the Direction did not name a person. Edit those and generate again; the script step is the cheapest step in the chain.

## Three hooks for one product

An invented product: a weighted sleep mask, for people who wake up at every sound and sliver of light.

Description: "A sleep mask with a soft, evenly weighted front that blocks all light and rests gently on the eyes. For people who wake up early from light or stress and cannot get back to sleep. Washable, fits any head."

Three hooks the script step might offer:

- "You don't wake up because you're rested." (wrong assumption)
- "Light sleepers, this one is for you." (named viewer)
- "Blocks every bit of light. Weighs almost nothing." (concrete detail)

All three fit the budget. The first hands off best to a problem line about light and stress; the second addresses the buyer most directly; the third is the plainest. Any of them would work, which is the point. By the time you are choosing between three decent hooks, the description has done its job.
