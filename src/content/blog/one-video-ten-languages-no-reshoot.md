You have one good video. A founder explaining the product, a presenter walking through the three things it does, a how-to that customers keep sharing. It works in one language, and you sell in six. The usual options are to reshoot it per market, to subtitle it and hope, or to dub it and accept that the mouth says something else. Lipsync translation adds a fourth: the same speaker, the same voice, now speaking each language with matching lips.

This article is about doing that well for many languages at once, using Clipelle's [Translate & lipsync](/platform/translate) as the concrete tool. If you want the mechanics of a single translation first, read [How lipsync translation works](/blog/how-lipsync-translation-works).

## The three usual routes and what they cost you

**Reshooting** gives the best result when you have a fluent presenter per language and the time to direct them. Most shops have neither, so the second-language version gets made months later, by someone else, with a different energy, and the two videos stop being the same ad.

**Subtitles** are cheap and keep the original voice. They also ask the viewer to read while a stranger speaks a language they do not know. On a phone feed, that is a lot to ask in the first second.

**Dubbing** replaces the voice. The viewer hears their language and watches a mouth that disagrees with it. Everyone has seen this and everyone tunes it out.

**Lipsync translation** keeps the speaker's own voice, moves the mouth with the new words and burns in captions. The viewer sees a person speaking to them. The cost is per language and per second, the time is minutes rather than weeks, and the result is the same ad in every market, which matters as soon as you want to compare how markets respond.

## Preparation that pays off ten times over

Anything wrong in the source is wrong in every language. An hour on the original saves ten rounds of fixes.

**Clean audio.** One speaker, clear speech, no voices talking over each other. If there is a music bed, keep it low under the voice; the translation has to hear the words.

**No burned-in text.** Captions or titles already in the source stay in the source language. Start from the version without them, and let Clipelle add captions per language.

**Short sentences, natural pauses.** Each translated sentence has to fit the time the original one took. Short sentences give the translation room; a breathless run-on gives it none.

**Names and numbers written down.** Put product names, feature names and taglines in the brand glossary so they survive unchanged in every language. Check the numbers you say out loud; a price or a size is the one thing that must be exact everywhere.

**Rights in order.** Before any translation starts, you confirm that you own the video and have the speaker's permission for their voice and face. If the speaker is a freelancer or an employee who has since left, settle that before you upload, not after.

**Within the limits.** Up to 32 MB and 3 minutes. A longer video is better cut into parts anyway; each part becomes a shorter ad.

## Picking the ten

Ten is a figure of speech. The right number is the number of markets where you can actually serve a customer: a localized store or checkout, shipping, support in that language. Translating a video for a country you cannot ship to is a nice demo and nothing else.

Within that set, regional variants are worth a thought. A language spoken in several countries is several audiences, with different words for the same things and different ideas of what sounds friendly. If the market is large enough to run ads in, it is large enough to deserve its own variant. [Which languages to localize first](/blog/which-languages-to-localize-first) walks through the ordering.

## Speed or Precision across many languages

The cost multiplies by the number of languages, so the Speed or Precision choice is worth making deliberately. Speed is standard lipsync and suits head-and-shoulders footage from a normal distance. Precision redraws the mouth, is slower and costs more, and shows its value when the face is large in the frame.

A sensible pattern: Precision for the one or two markets that will carry most of the budget, Speed for the markets you are testing. If a test market takes off, re-run that one language on Precision. The estimate for the whole set, in credits and dollars, is on the form before you start, and the real amount per version is on the project page afterwards.

## The quality check at scale

Every translated line is compared with the original before the paid render: missing or added words, a changed meaning, a wrong number, a mangled name, unnatural phrasing. Corrections are made within the timing of each line, and the changes and warnings are listed per language on the project page.

That check is what makes ten languages manageable by one person. What it does not replace is a native read the first time you add a language. Register and politeness, whether the video speaks to the viewer formally or casually, is a judgment call a check can flag but not make for you. Our [localization checklist](/blog/localization-checklist-for-product-videos) lists what that first native viewer should look for; after the first video in a language, the check carries most of the load.

## The workflow, start to finish

1. Upload the clean source video and confirm the rights.
2. Set the spoken language or let it be detected.
3. Pick the target languages and variants, several at once.
4. Fill in the brand glossary.
5. Choose Speed or Precision, caption position, 9:16 or original, enhancement.
6. Read the estimate, start, and leave the page. The project page shows each step per language.
7. Download each version from the project page and check names and numbers once.

If translating is something you do every week, the same run is available through Clipelle's API and its MCP server, so an assistant or a script can start a translation from a file link and read back the results without opening the form.

## An example: one 45-second product video, five markets

Say the source is a 45-second founder video in English, filmed on a phone at arm's length, going to Germany, France, Spain, Italy and the Netherlands.

- Source check: one speaker, no on-screen titles, the three product names in the glossary, the one price spoken as a round number.
- Languages: German, French, Spanish, Italian, Dutch.
- Mode: Speed for all five; the face is small enough in the frame. Precision later for whichever market gets the main budget.
- Format: 9:16 for the feeds, captions low.
- After the run: five versions on the project page, the quality check's corrections per language, one native friend per language for the first watch.

The reshoot that would have replaced this is five presenters, five shoots and five edits. The translation is one upload and one estimate, and the five ads are the same ad.
